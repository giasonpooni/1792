# Reference corpus synthesis — 2026-10-01

The recent visual batches are now large enough to support a stable **direct-craft brief** for 1792 before industrial workflows begin producing large amounts of content.

This document records the high-value conclusions that existed in conversation but were not yet in the repository. It is deliberately conservative: later photographs, paintings, modern renders and studio images are useful only inside their provenance tier.

## 1. World scale should be legible before detail

The strongest recurring architectural references support a hierarchy of readable scales:

- **city/fort scale:** wall mass, skyline interruption, gate sequence, causeway/approach;
- **court scale:** axial open space, arcade perimeter, water/drain line, audience threshold;
- **room scale:** repeated arch frames, panel fields, screened openings, daylight endpoint;
- **human scale:** raised plinths, shade edges, hand-contact surfaces, waiting places;
- **centimetre scale:** repairs, polish, rope wear, replaced slats, faded cloth, mismatched plaster.

The future generator may create abundance at every scale, but must preserve these cross-scale relationships.

## 2. Dense edges / dynamic center remains the urban rule

The reference corpus repeatedly supports a street section where occupation accumulates at the edges and the center changes over time.

```text
open lane
→ pedestrians
→ horse
→ cart
→ quarrel
→ procession
→ cleared authority route
```

A dense city therefore does not require a permanently saturated center.

## 3. Thresholds are events, not holes in walls

Repeated references support:

```text
compression / shade
→ deep framed opening
→ controlled glimpse
→ brighter court / garden / water / authority
```

Threshold direction should consider who waits, who is waved through, what can be glimpsed before entry, where guards stand, which surfaces are touched repeatedly, and whether the space immediately beyond expands or compresses.

This is especially important for Ranjit's life course: the same gate should treat Buddh, a young sardār and the Maharaja differently.

## 4. Courtyard occupation should overlay formal geometry

Formal plans may be symmetrical while daily use is not. Hand-author asymmetries such as one shaded repair place, one preferred waiting edge, one water vessel kept near a threshold, one bench used by clerks, one drain segment repaired later, and one arcade bay consistently less occupied.

This avoids making every formal courtyard feel museum-like.

## 5. Ornament needs a density ladder

Do not expose a single ornament-amount parameter. A more useful hierarchy is:

```text
plain structural frame
→ carved border
→ panel field
→ painted/floral layer
→ reflective/inlaid layer
→ textile/furniture layer
```

Different rooms stop at different levels. Ranjit's environment can become more ornamented as his authority grows without making his own body the most visually extravagant element in every scene.

## 6. Important people need behavioral silhouette

Across later portrait and court references, hand occupation and posture repeatedly matter as much as costume.

Important characters should eventually receive a small set of durable habits: preferred hand near sword or sash, seated-forward vs seated-back listening posture, habitual document placement, where attendants leave space around them, how they cross a threshold, and how a familiar object is handled.

These habits should survive costume changes across decades.

## 7. Equipment should be asymmetrical

Avoid modern inventory-slot symmetry. A memorable armed silhouette may have sword and pouch heavy on one side, cloth knot overlapping the belt, shield resting elsewhere, bow or staff changing the vertical silhouette, and one side of the torso visually open.

Irregularity is a character-design asset.

## 8. Daily-life animation should be occupation-specific

A vessel, grain load, timber bundle, saddle, child, wounded person or weapon changes the whole body. Do not solve this as `neutral walk + prop parented to hand`.

Direct-craft motion families should account for balance, step width, stopping, switching sides, setting objects down, and fatigue.

## 9. Sacred / monumental approach can remain powerful without enterable interiors

The existing project rule remains valuable. Exterior experience can include crowd slowing, shade transition, shoes/preparation where historically and religiously appropriate, water/reflection, vendors/attendants, waiting relatives, sound, and visual reveal.

Restricted interiors need not become gameplay dungeons.

## 10. Material continuity is story continuity

The corpus now justifies explicitly preserving repairs of different ages, wear concentrated where hands actually touch, remounted ornaments, replacement metal/wood, rotated or re-used furnishings, faded surfaces protected by belts/folds, and objects retained after a person is absent.

The point is not procedural dirt. The point is **time becoming visible**.

## 11. Ranjit visual continuity

The adult Ranjit direction remains anchored separately from the modern armored-ruler renders in the supplement.

Modern armored imagery is **D-tier only**. It may help answer staging questions such as body/chair/weapon overlap or close material readability. It may not define Ranjit's historical armor, face, throne, helmet or costume.

The durable Ranjit thesis remains:

> Power grows faster than personal spectacle.

His court, gifts, textiles, retainers and political environment can accumulate splendor faster than his own ornament.

## 12. Industrialization contract

Before a future workflow generates content, it should receive:

1. reference tier;
2. allowed visual influences;
3. forbidden historical inferences;
4. geometric/spatial grammar;
5. authored memory anchors;
6. habitual occupation constraints;
7. gameplay clearance envelope.

The workflow produces candidates.

Human/direct craft retains authority over meaningful exception, historical judgment, final silhouette, performance, composition, beauty, and what remains unexplained.

That is the boundary this pre-industrial phase is intended to establish.
